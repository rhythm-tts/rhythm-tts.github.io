Rhythm-TTS: High-Fidelity Shortcut Latent Modeling and Probabilistic Rhythm Generation for Zero-Shot Text-to-Speech
Code; Paper

AbstractZero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) systems are commonly built on either discrete codec tokens or continuous acoustic representations. Discrete-token models benefit from stable sequence modeling and explicit speech factorization, but they ultimately synthesize speech through codebook lookup and codeword composition, which can accumulate errors and impose a quality ceiling on naturalness and speaker similarity. Continuous-representation models can generate fine-grained speech latents directly, but often require heavier in-context modeling and less explicit alignment. This paper presents \textbf{Rhythm-TTS}, a shortcut zero-shot TTS framework that combines the stability of discrete codec tokens with the fidelity of continuous latent generation. Instead of using predicted codec tokens as the final acoustic representation, Rhythm-TTS uses them as a semantic-acoustic prior and directly generates entangled continuous speech latents with flow matching, bypassing the discrete-to-latent reconstruction path at inference time. We further address an underexplored dimension of speech naturalness: rhythm diversity across multiple synthesis runs. Motivated by the hypothesis that rhythm variation is largely governed by phoneme duration changes and the occurrence of silent pauses, we introduce probabilistic Duration and Silence Generators to sample dynamic pacing patterns before acoustic generation. Experiments on zero-shot TTS benchmarks evaluate intelligibility, naturalness, speaker similarity, efficiency, and run-to-run rhythm diversity, showing that the proposed shortcut latent modeling and probabilistic rhythm generation improve the fidelity and temporal naturalness of non-autoregressive zero-shot TTS. Code and audio samples are available at our demo page.

Model Overview

Figure 1: Overview of Rhythm-TTS. The input speech prompt is first processed by the Codec Encoder, which produces six latent sequences: one for prosody, two for content, and three for acoustic details. These encoded representations are then duplicated based on the durations predicted by the Duration Generator, while the Silence Generator inserts silences after each phoneme. The Code Decoder then generates predicted codes for the text prompt, conditioned on both the encoded phonemes and the latent representation of the reference speech. These predicted codes are converted into embeddings and merged before being passed through the Denoiser, where flow matching is performed. Finally, the output embeddings are fed into the Codec Decoder to synthesize the final speech waveform.

Figure 2: Code Decoder architecture. With the encoded phonemes, the codes are generated gradually by corresponding FFT Blocks with condition of prior code of speech prompt. These synthesized codes are combined by a CNN module. This embedding is finally finetuned by the Denoiser with flow matching algorithm.
